A label is not a choice

A label is not a choice

YouTube Music and Deezer are starting to answer a simple question: if a listener does not want generated music, can they avoid it?

A label helps. YouTube Music is rolling out labels for fully generated, partly generated and non-generated music. That is better than pretending the question does not exist. But a tiny note on a player page arrives after the recommendation system, the playlists and the search results have already done their work.

The useful thing is a filter. Deezer says it is building one, so people can hide fully AI-generated tracks, albums and related artists from search. That is the honest version of choice. Want the machine-made stuff? Fine. Do not want it in your listening? Also fine.

The category needs to be narrow enough not to become stupid. Routine production tools—editing, pitch correction, noise removal—should not make a song “AI music.” The meaningful line is substantial generated authorship: a generated voice, performance, composition or lyrics doing a real part of the creative work.

That line will be messy. Platforms cannot reliably infer authorship from a finished audio file, and creators may not disclose everything. So the labels should say, in effect, whether they are based on a creator declaration, automated detection, or both. Do not dress a guess up as a fact.

This is not an argument that generated music is automatically bad. Some will be interesting, and most music made by people is not sacred simply because people made it. The problem is volume and control. Deezer said it was detecting roughly 90,000 fully AI-generated uploads a day in July. At that scale, a label alone is decorative.

Streaming services already decide what gets placed in front of people. Giving listeners a clean way to opt out is not censorship. It is a basic setting, like an explicit-content switch, except less morally panicked.

A label tells you what you are hearing. A filter lets you decide whether to hear it at all.
