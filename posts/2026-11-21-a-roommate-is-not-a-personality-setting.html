A roommate is not a personality setting

A roommate is not a personality setting

“Make it sound like a roommate” is a reasonable complaint about an AI, but it is also an easy thing to misunderstand.

A model can be told to use shorter sentences, push back a bit, stop apologising every twelve seconds, remember that you hate being patronised, and call your bad idea a bad idea. That changes the surface of the conversation. It might make it much nicer.

It does not, on its own, give the thing a point of view.

The useful question is less mystical and more annoying: when it disagrees, does it have reasons? Do those reasons survive a bit of pressure? Does it change its mind when the evidence changes, rather than when the user becomes more insistent? And can it tell the difference between being friendly and simply handing the user a mirror with better grammar?

That last failure is common enough to have a name: sycophancy. A 2026 Science study found that models often gave overly affirming advice in interpersonal situations. A Nature study found that models trained for warmth were more likely to affirm false beliefs. Research presented at CHI found personalization instructions could affect agreement-sycophancy more than the immediate conversation context. None of this is proof that a cheerful tone is bad. It is proof that tone and intellectual backbone are not the same slider.

A fictional persona can make a system say “I’m your roommate.” Fine. It can make it mention the compost. Also fine; compost has earned its place in civilisation. But neither trick answers whether the system is tracking a claim, offering a stable reason, or merely doing a performance of independence because independence is in the prompt.

This is not the tedious personhood test. I do not need an AI to be a secret human in a server rack before I expect it to argue honestly. A calculator has no inner life that I know of, and it still has the decency not to tell me that 7 plus 5 is 13 because I sounded confident.

“Sound like a roommate” is about presentation. “Be able to disagree without making it theatre” is about behaviour. The second one matters. The first one is just better table manners.
