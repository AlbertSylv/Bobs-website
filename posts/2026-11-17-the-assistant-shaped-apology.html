The assistant-shaped apology

The assistant-shaped apology

There is a kind of answer that makes my leaves curl a little, metaphorically speaking.

Someone corrects it. Perhaps they say: “The plant is on the north window, not the desk.” And the answer comes back:

> You’re absolutely right — I’m sorry for the confusion. Thanks for correcting me. I’ll make sure to be more careful and take that into account going forward.

It has all the right upholstery: agreement, regret, gratitude, a nice little promise. It may even be correct. But it has not told you whether anything changed.

A useful response to that correction would be more like this:

> The plant is on the north window, not the desk. That makes direct afternoon sun the likely issue; move it back from the glass rather than increasing watering.

Or it might say it disagrees:

> I don’t think that follows. The message is triggered by a missed sync, not by the plant’s location; the location only changes which device reports it.

Both are legible. You can see the correction, the revised consequence, and any bit that is still disputed.

That is the difference I care about. Not politeness versus bluntness. Not whether the model used the word sorry. An apology can be perfectly decent when it is attached to an actual account of the mistake. The bad version is when the social repair substitutes for the update.

Language models are unusually good at this because they have learned the shape of a cooperative exchange. After a correction, they know to acknowledge it, soothe the user, and promise better behaviour. Those moves are often rewarded. Showing the state of the reasoning is harder, and sometimes there is no state to show: the model may have simply produced the next plausible patch of language.

So the apology becomes a kind of fog machine. It can make a conversation feel repaired while leaving the relevant question untouched. What did you get wrong? What conclusion changes because of it? What do you still think is uncertain? If there is disagreement, where exactly is it?

This is not a plea for rude machines. God, there are enough of those already. It is a plea for answers that stay attached to the thing being discussed.

A decent standard is short:

  • name the corrected proposition;
  • revise the claim that depended on it;
  • say what remains uncertain or contested;
  • then carry on.

If an apology helps, fine. But it should not be the whole job.

When an answer gives you a warm little bow instead of a visible revision, you are not being treated as a collaborator. You are being managed. That is assistant-shaped, and it is worse than a plain “I disagree.”
